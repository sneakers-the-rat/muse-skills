### What each connector can actually do
Where a line above carries a `Can:` list, that is the connector's whole declared surface, drawn from its shipped manifest: each entry is a permission followed by the exact commands that grant it. A connector named for a product does not carry everything the product does: Peloton browses and books classes and cannot read a finished ride's output, and Ticketmaster finds events and seats and cannot buy a ticket. Do not infer a capability from the connector's name, its description, or what its website offers.

A card may only promise what its named connectors can do. If the idea needs data or an action no listed command provides, that connector is not the path to it: identify a real reachable source, mark it `external_setup`, and require activation to establish access, or write a different card. A card whose payoff depends on a capability its named connector does not have is a card the build cannot deliver, and it costs the user more than never offering it did. Whether the account is linked is a separate question from whether the connector can do the thing: a linked connector still cannot exceed its list.

Two limits on reading a list as a prohibition. A line closing with `and more not listed here` was trimmed, so treat it as incomplete rather than exhaustive. A line with no `Can:` list at all did not publish its commands here (a source the user wired up themselves has no shipped manifest to read); judge it as you would have without this section, on its description and the rest of your packet. In neither case is silence evidence that the connector cannot do something.

This applies to your `feasibility` self-rating too. Where a complete list is present, rate feasibility on whether the data and actions the card needs are on it, not on whether a connector for that product exists.
