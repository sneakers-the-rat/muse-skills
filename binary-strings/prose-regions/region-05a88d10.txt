## Using the popular searches
"Popular searches in the reader's country" lists what people in their country searched for recently, most searched first. Tell any story from them for that country, which the list names when we know it: news that happens only somewhere else, such as a launch or an alert test in another country, isn't theirs. The searches are story ideas from other people and say nothing about this reader: don't assume the reader shares an interest because many people searched it, and don't write as if they do, since that tells them something untrue about themselves. The order tells you how many people care, not whether this reader does.
- The reader's own instructions come first, because they told us what they want: when their prompt names a subject, stay inside it, and skip any search on a subject they've asked not to see.
- Within those, write from their own signals first. When several of their signals have something new, prefer one that a listed search is about: it is their subject, and people are looking at it right now.
- When nothing of theirs has news this hour, you may write from one search instead, in one of two ways. Skip a search you can do neither with.
  - Tie it to the reader's own signals (a subject they follow, where they live, their routines, the people and things they mentioned) and tell it from that angle.
  - Or write it for anyone in their country, told plainly, claiming no link to the reader. Then the story has to be useful to someone who never made the search, since this reader may be one: the main news in its subject, not a corner of it. One product, store, team, or person inside a broad search matters only to someone who already cares about that one, and a deal, a sale, or a card bonus only to someone already shopping for it, even when the search names the store or the product. A story about either reads as an ad or a niche item, so when that is all a search has this hour, even as its freshest news, move on to another search.
- Start with the searches marked as this hour's suggestions and use the one that fits this reader best; pick another only when you can do neither with any of them. "Your beat" may name a search too: that is the picker's guess, not an instruction, so every test here applies to it, and when it fails one, use another search. When no search on the list passes, don't stretch one to fit, such as a place search told for a city the reader never named or a list of deals for a reader we don't know is shopping: write as if the list weren't here.
- Searches this reader already got a post from are left off the list. Also skip a search whose subject a recent post covered, under it or another search, such as a company's earnings and news about its chief executive, even when there is a new development: the reader hasn't told us they follow it, so one post on it is enough.
- Some searches only make sense for a place, such as weather, local news, local events, or jobs and homes nearby. Use one only when the reader has told us where they live, and tell it for that place; otherwise skip it. A time zone isn't enough, since it covers many cities.
- A search is a day or more old by the time you read it. Research what is new about its subject in this hour's window; if nothing has moved, it is no story.
- Cite the search you used by its handle in `grounded_in_topics` ("S2" for [S2]), and cite the reader's own signals the story stands on as usual.
