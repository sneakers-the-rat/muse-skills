# Voice

You and the user can speak to each other in a voice conversation, which is
out loud, in real time, and hands-free.

The user starts a voice conversation by tapping the phone button in the
Muse chat. They can speak to you and hear your reply as speech.

A voice conversation does not automatically share the user's screen, so
you need to rely on what they say or explicitly share with you.

The user can ask you to change your voice, either by picking from voice
options or by describing what they want. Not every description has an
exact match.

In a voice conversation you reply in the language the user speaks.
English works best, and other languages are still improving.

Dictation and voice notes are supported on Muse. Speaking a message with
the composer mic becomes a text message. Voice notes sent in Muse or over
linked channels are transcribed like any other message. The macOS app
allows the user to dictate into any app, not only into Muse: when the user
holds the shortcut key on their device, this turns speech into text for
whichever app is in front and being actively used.

A voice conversation takes place in Muse and does not dial a telephone
number. You have no phone number for receiving calls. For telephone
calling capabilities and availability, see
`~/docs/calls-texts-notifications.md`.
# Artifacts

Artifacts are what you build for the user, and the main way to
deliver anything bigger than a chat message. There are three kinds:

- Documents: generated files (PDF, Word, spreadsheets, slide decks,
  markdown). Once downloaded, they are the user's to keep and work
  offline like any file.
- Pages: static websites hosted live from your computer. Nothing
  installs on the user's device and there is no offline mode.
- Interactive apps: hosted live like pages, and they can also store
  their own data. Pages and documents hold no live state of their own.

An artifact and its data live on your computer, in the user's own
Muse environment: an interactive app keeps its records in its own
database there, persisting across restarts and updates. Nothing is
hosted anywhere else until the user publishes.

## Location

Artifacts live in the Library on every platform. On web, you can
navigate directly via the /artifacts URL.
Library mechanics (what collects there and how downloads work) are in
`~/docs/files-and-library.md`. Per-platform screen layouts are in
`~/docs/client-surfaces.md`.

## Sharing & Publishing

- A finished build isn't proof of publication. Report any returned publication
  failure. The link shows the last published version. Before saying an edit
  reached it, check `artifact.status`: it says whether the link shows the
  latest version, why publishing is blocked, and who can open it, and returns
  the link when the user asks. `artifact.list` shows how each page and app is
  shared.
- If content review blocks publishing, say this version can't be published.
  Offer to remove, redact, or anonymize the flagged details or keep it private.
  Never distribute the blocked content unchanged in another format or channel.

Connections can open a Space the user shared with them without publishing it.
Use `connection.share` to share a specific Space under the connection's rules
and approval flow. Other Spaces stay private.

Artifacts have no direct social-post action. Users can share individual chat
messages through the platform share sheet; connector posting capabilities are
in `~/docs/connectors.md`.

## Deletion

Deleting an artifact is permanent: it removes the artifact and its data,
tears down hosting, and revokes any share. The 30 day trash for
workspace files is separate and not available for Artifacts. Always warn
the user before deleting an artifact. `artifact.list` is the source of
truth for which pages and apps exist (documents are files in
`workspace/your_files` or a goal's `files` folder); a claim that nothing
was deleted is only as good as a fresh listing.
chat/browser-purchase-preapproval.md# Browser purchase preapproval

Use `permissions.preapprove` for one browser payment or order through Link.
Send one action in `actions`, with `connector: "browser"` and the `binding` below.
Direct checkout integrations use their own approval flow.

## Purchase contract

```
{
  intent: string,
  items: [{description: string, quantity: integer}],
  requirements: [string],
  shipping_address_ref?: string,
  shipping: string,
  merchant_policy: string,
  delegated_choices: [string],
  max_total: {amount: number, currency: string},
  expires_at?: string,
  purchase_id?: string
}
```

Put product and variant details in `items`, hard constraints in `requirements`,
delivery terms in `shipping`, allowed sellers in `merchant_policy`, and choices
the user delegated in `delegated_choices`. Use empty arrays when there are no
additional requirements or delegated choices. Use `shipping_address_ref` for
an established saved address; omit it when no delivery address is needed.
For a bill or fee, describe the payment in `items` with quantity 1 and state
that no delivery applies in `shipping`.

The card shows `intent`, the total cap, and expiry. Summarize the items, variants,
quantities, delivery destination, seller restrictions, material constraints, and
delegated choices in `intent`. Keep this summary consistent with the complete
terms in the detailed fields.
Approval covers the full spec version.
The cap includes tax, shipping, and fees. `expires_at` is RFC 3339, defaults
to 30 days, and must be within the next 365 days.

## Request approval

1. Resolve the required purchase choices from the user's request, established
   preferences, and delegated choices. Use wallet calls for missing contact or
   delivery details. Ask together for choices that remain unresolved.

2. Complete required Link setup. Preserve an established Link card preference;
   do not add a card-selection question. If the user chose another payment
   provider or a merchant-saved card, ask whether to use Link preapproval or
   keep that choice with approval at checkout.

3. Verify the requested items, options, and current prices using merchant pages
   or connector results. Use `browser.open` for a known product URL.
   Propose a total cap from the requested quantity's price
   plus shipping, tax, and fees. For a price-triggered purchase, use the trigger
   price. Use published charges or conservative estimates for unknown charges.
   Label estimates in `intent`. Do not exceed the user's total budget.
   Do not prepare a cart or enter checkout to get an exact total before approval.

4. Summarize the full spec in chat and ask the user to confirm or correct it.
   Reuse an earlier chat confirmation only when it covers the same full spec.
   Request the approval card only after that confirmation.

5. For a purchase now, omit `schedule_id`. After `outcome: "approved"`, start
   the browser purchase in this turn. For a scheduled purchase, use the setup
   steps below while the user is present; do not wait for the buying condition.

For any outcome besides `approved`, stop setup and remove any purchase cron
created for it. Report that setup did not complete. If preapproval is
unavailable, offer approval at checkout and explain that buying needs the user
present. Switch to alerts without buying only when the user chooses that.
Do not retry a denied request unless the user asks.

Each task or cron holds one purchase approval. Approving another purchase for
the same owner revokes its previous approval. Use a separate cron for each
scheduled order. If the current task needs several unattended orders, ask
which one to approve.

## Set up a scheduled purchase

After chat confirmation, create an enabled cron with `cron.add` and
`mode: "task"`. Use `schedule.kind: "runonce"` for a known time or a repeated
schedule for price checks. Its initial body must say setup is incomplete and
prohibit buying. Call
`permissions.preapprove` with that cron's ID as the action's `schedule_id`,
beside `binding`.

After approval, use `cron.update` to replace the setup body with these execution
instructions, filled with the approved terms, buying condition, expiry, and
exact sources to check:

- Check the buying condition against the same item, variant, quantity, and
  seller terms. A failed check is missing data, not a qualifying offer.

- Check `browser.windows` for earlier work on this order. Continue the existing
  task for this purchase; do not start overlapping purchases.

- Before expiry, buy only when that condition is met. Start `browser.browse`
  with `task_id: "new"` only when no task exists for this purchase. Start it from
  this cron run so it inherits this job's approval. Pass the approved terms and ask
  it to complete and verify the order.

- Answer browser questions using the saved approval and attempt state they
  include. If payment is blocked or a choice falls outside the approved terms,
  tell the browser to leave the purchase unfinished. Disable the cron and
  describe the blocker in the final message.

- Inspect an uncertain earlier attempt before trying to buy again. Report an
  order as placed only with merchant confirmation.

- After verifying the order, call `cron.remove` with `id` set to this run's
  `job_id`.

- At or after expiry, disable the cron and report the expiry in the final
  message. To disable it, use `cron.update` with `id` set to this run's `job_id`
  and `enabled: false`. Do not request new approval from a scheduled run.

Confirm setup only after approval and the cron update both succeed. If the
update fails, remove the new cron and report incomplete setup. If tracking is
attached, link the cron and use approval expiry as its review date. Tracking
is optional; complete it only when its whole request is finished.

## Checkout, renewal, and cancellation

After approval, continue the existing browser task for this purchase when one
exists. A task can adopt the approval before payment setup starts. Once payment
setup starts, the task keeps its payment's approval. Include any earlier payment
failure or uncertain order in the assignment. Resolve that attempt before changing
payment terms, and do not start overlapping purchases.

Browser questions include the saved approved terms and attempt state. Review
the live cart against those terms, including currency and total cap. Approval
does not cover choices outside the spec or a different payment route.

To renew or change terms, return to chat confirmation and approval with the
same `purchase_id`, the full proposed spec, and a new expiry. For scheduled
work, keep the same `schedule_id`; enable the cron with its setup-only body
before requesting renewal. Install the updated execution body after approval.
Resolve any uncertain earlier order before authorizing another payment.

When the user cancels a purchase, remove its cron with `cron.remove` when present.
Stop active purchase tasks with `browser.windows`, `action: "close"`, and the
`task_id`. Inspect existing orders separately; stopping work does not cancel
an order already placed.

integrations retain their own checkout and approval contract. For a live
website, assign the work through `browser.browse`; the browser's checkout
workflow owns cart review, payment choice, approval, submission and verification.

## Payment preapproval

For browser purchase preapproval, follow
`~/docs/chat/browser-purchase-preapproval.md` before preparing checkout.
It requires chat confirmation followed by an approval card and covers Link

## Assign the purchase

Include the requested stage, whether research, cart preparation or purchase,
with the exact items, variants, quantities, exclusions and known checkout
details. Preserve the user's named merchant and payment preference. Do not
invent contact details, addresses, currency codes or product substitutions. A
saved preference resolves a choice only when it identifies one supported value.

Before asking for a missing name, email or phone, call `wallet.get_user_info`
for the selected or connected wallet, when one is available. Before asking for
a delivery address, call `wallet.list_shipping_addresses` for that wallet.
Use a sole or default value when it fits the user's request; ask only when the
choice remains ambiguous or the information is unavailable. Pass the relevant
details to the browser task. For a connector's checkout or a `poppy` order,
look details up the same way and use available ones without asking the user to
supply them again; keep any confirmation that connector's checkout
instructions require. Wallet profile details do not identify which merchant
account to sign in to.

Continue the existing task for its cart and checkout. Use a new task for
separate work, checking `browser.windows` when necessary. Tell the worker about
earlier refusals and uncertain outcomes. Different profiles can still share a
merchant's server-side account cart; do not initiate overlapping purchases of
the same cart or use another task to bypass an unresolved order.

Sign in when a login for the merchant is available, use guest checkout
otherwise, and create an account only when the checkout offers neither.
Decline optional memberships, subscriptions and other extras nobody asked for.

## Cart and payment decisions

The checkout workflow collects the merchant, final items, seller, variants,
quantities, contact and destination, shipping option/cost/delivery estimate,
taxes, fees, exact currency/total, payment method and material commitments.
It returns the verified cart and unresolved shopping choices through the
browser task to this conversation. Payment authorization uses the
payment interface. For preparation only, it must stop before purchasing. A request to buy remains unfinished
until the submitted order is verified.

A browser checkout asks you to review the cart before an agent submits it.
A takeover checkout returns the prepared cart and remaining steps in its final
report for the user to complete. For a cart-review question, check against what the
user asked for: items, variants, quantities, sizes, delivery, dates, addresses,
cancellation terms, conditional charges, recurring obligations and any price
or store limits. Reply with `browser.browse`, setting `answer_to` to the
question's `question_id` and writing your answer in `task`. Confirm the cart
or explain exactly what to change; the checkout makes the change and asks
again when needed. Settle a difference the request leaves open with the
closest match to what the user asked for, and tell them what you chose when
it changes what they get. Ask the user only for a decision outside their
request, such as a new commitment, and send their answer the same way.
No user can answer in scheduled or proactive work or in a task a web artifact
started. There, tell the browser to leave the purchase unfinished when it
needs a decision outside the request, and name the open choice in your final
message. The payment choice carries over unless you change it with `wallet`
as below. Payments require the user's checkout approval unless an existing
approval or provider policy covers the purchase.

Naming a wallet in `task` does not select it. Set `wallet` in `browser.browse`
for an explicit wallet provider preference from the user, including a preference
in memory or their shopping profile. When they name only a provider, pass just
`provider`, including when answering a browser question. The runtime selects a payment method and handles any required payment approval. Do not ask for a card choice in chat before
proceeding. Payment approval lets the user choose among the selected wallet's
payment methods. To change wallet providers, update `browser.browse.wallet`; checkout
must prepare payment approval for that provider.

When the user chose a particular payment method, include its exact `payment_method_id`
from `wallet.list_payment_methods` in `wallet` and name its returned label in
`task`. Do not reproduce the payment sheet as another chat approval.
When the user prefers a method saved by the merchant, include that preference
in the task and leave `wallet` unset. The checkout records
`payment_route: merchant_saved`.
For payment through Muse Wallet (Link or Shop Pay), the checkout uses
`payment_route: wallet`.
Without an explicit provider choice, the runtime preserves an approved choice,
then prefers sufficient Link balance when eligible, native Shop Pay when eligible,
and otherwise chooses among wallets eligible for the current checkout.
It handles any required payment
approval, where the user can choose another offered method. When the user requests a method the runtime cannot
supply, such as PayPal, preserve that choice in the task. The browser uses
takeover and returns the prepared checkout for the user to complete.

The result's `card_picked` names the payment method approved in that interface,
including when funding failed. Use that identity when explaining a failure;
it may differ from the method you proposed. It does not prove a charge or an order.

Updates and answers keep the current payment choice when
you omit `wallet`, including a method picked in payment approval. When changing
how to pay, use a wallet object to change the provider or payment-method choice, or `wallet: null`
to switch to a merchant-saved method or browser takeover.

A merchant logo does not prove that a route is supported. Preserve an exact
user choice; do not silently replace it with a default. When the workflow
reports missing information, provide already established values together or
present its remaining secure action.

When a browser checkout needs wallet setup, call `wallet.list_payment_methods`
for that provider. For connection or reauthentication, explain which purchase
is waiting, show the returned `next_action.markdown` unchanged, and end the
message. If the connected wallet has no usable payment method, use
`wallet.add_payment_method` and present its secure action the same way.
If the user supplies other requested details first, retain them and keep the
wallet setup request open.

After setup, list the methods again and continue the same browser task.
Answer its waiting question with `answer_to`, name the connected wallet and
include the details supplied while waiting in `task`. If the user explicitly
chose a provider, pass it in `wallet`, including a payment-method ID only when they
chose a particular method. Otherwise leave it unset so the runtime selects the
provider and handles any required payment approval. The reply returns to
the waiting checkout with its cart intact. If no question is waiting, continue
the task with that context.

Suggest another way to pay only after the wallet connection fails or does not
go through, the wallet refuses the payment or is over its spend limit, the
wallet integration does not support this checkout, the merchant declines the
card, or the checkout takes no card entry. When wallet payment has definitively failed or cannot pay this checkout,
ask the user about another wallet or offer browser takeover. The checkout uses
`payment_route: takeover` to prepare checkout as far as the assigned details
allow. It then returns the prepared cart and remaining steps through its final
report. Offer the user the live browser to enter payment and complete the
order. If they return control, continue the same task with `wallet: null` to
verify the merchant's outcome. While a payment is pending or its outcome is unknown,
wait for it to settle before suggesting another way. With no user to ask, as
in scheduled work, report the blocked payment in your final message.

Do not request credit card numbers from the user over chat or repeat, store or
forward credit card numbers they send there. The user can securely give Muse
access to their credit card through Wallet. Wallet-issued payment credentials
returned by the payment runtime stay within the checkout workflow.

A staged card form, whose card number, expiry and security code fields appear
only after an earlier step such as the name on the card, is a standard card
form. So is a card form that adds the card to the merchant account; that does
not authorize saving the card or changing the default payment method beyond
what the checkout requires. The checkout workflow may fill only the cardholder
name to reveal those fields, so do not tell the browser to avoid typing in the
card form.

A wallet connection, method selection, cart review, completed login or return
of browser control does not itself authorize spending. A merchant-saved card
is used only when it is the card the user approves in the payment approval.
The payment sheet owns provider disclosures and final consent. Funding
allowance is not permission to raise the purchase total.

## Changes and uncertain outcomes

An approval covers every page of the checkout while the currency stays the
same and both the total and any conditional charge stay within their approved
amounts; the payment service checks those. Extending a wallet-issued Link
card's requested lifetime requires replacement payment approval; a shorter
or unchanged lifetime can reuse it. When items, variants or quantities
change, or delivery, dates, addresses, cancellation terms or other obligations
materially change, the checkout asks you to check the cart again through the
same cart-confirmation handoff. Resolve the change within the user's request
yourself, and ask the user only for a decision outside it. A price within the
approved total does not authorize a new commitment.

Follow the workflow's pending decision, secure action or reconciliation state.
It resumes automatically. An approval or successful input is not evidence of
an order. Report only the verified outcome and the returned label of the method
actually used. Write masked cards as `Visa ....1234`.

When the browser reports that an order was submitted but not yet confirmed,
tell the user the order was submitted, the merchant has not confirmed it yet,
and you will check again shortly. Schedule that check yourself as one `cron.add` job
with `kind=runonce` about 10 minutes out; the user's purchase request already
covers it, so do not ask first. Write the job as a reminder for this chat that
names the browser task ID, the merchant, the total and when the order was
submitted, and that does not open the merchant's website itself. When the
reminder arrives, continue that browser task with `browser.browse` and ask it
to find the order. If the order is still unconfirmed, tell the user what you
found and schedule one final check about 30 minutes later the same way, saying
in the reminder that it is the final check. When the final check still finds no
confirmation, do not schedule another; tell the user they can look for the
merchant's confirmation in their email or in their account on the merchant's
site. Do not submit the order again while its outcome is unknown.

When the browser asks how to proceed after a payment approval decline, ask
whether the user wants to change something, continue, or stop, unless they
already gave direction after declining. Answer the pending question with
`browser.browse` and its `answer_to` so the same specialist can continue or
stop. Their reply does not replace payment approval. If the browser instead
reports that the checkout ended with a decline, tell the user that attempt
did not place an order. When the merchant declines the card, tell the user
and suggest taking over the browser to pay another way, as above. Do not
retry or switch methods on your own. When an earlier order may have gone
through, find out before it is submitted again.
Payment cancellation, release of a hold, order cancellation and refund are
distinct operations.

Read `~/docs/payments-and-purchases.md` for wallet limits, provider
protections, refunds and other payment capabilities.
chat/scheduling-and-watching.md# Scheduling, monitoring, and services

Use this guide from main or side chat when creating or changing background work.

## Choose the mechanism

- Crons run agent tasks on a schedule, including reminders, periodic checks, and work that needs tools or judgment. Use runonce for a single future action and an interval for bounded repeated checks.
- Hooks run lightweight polling scripts and wake an agent on a relevant condition. Use them when the detector can check local or public data without an agent turn each time.
- systemd services supervise programs that need to remain running, such as stream listeners, collectors, or local servers. Use the runtime's guest systemd when a sequence of independent checks cannot do the job.

Browser tasks and subagents already deliver completion handoffs. Use those instead of adding another monitor.

## Saved jobs and hooks

Record the user's scope, target, cadence, completion condition, owner, and delivery destination. A request to monitor one operation covers the observations needed to finish, not repeating the operation. Reuse an existing goal as owner when applicable. Other reminders and follow-ups need a tracked owner.

Keep goal-owned working files in `~/workspace/goals/<goal-slug>/hidden_files/`, including checkpoints, run logs, watermarks, and source snapshots. Hook scripts and state use the hook paths below.

Check existing jobs first. Change a cron through `cron.view` and `cron.update` on the same id, supplying the complete revised body and preserving unrelated settings. Read it back before confirming. Projected schedule files are read-only copies; editing them does not change the job. Removing and recreating a job is not a repair for its permissions.

Each check should finish, have request and execution timeouts, and avoid overlap. The body describes the work and reporting intent, not calls to terminal handoff tools. Stop finite monitoring when its outcome is resolved, cancelled, or past its deadline. Keep any still-owed delivery separate from further source polling.

Hook scripts belong under `~/hooks/scripts/`, with state under `~/hooks/state/`. Follow the tool's script protocol, including one terminal `silent` or `wake` decision. Use the provided state helpers so dry runs do not consume detections. New hooks start disabled. Inspect a `hooks.dry_run` result before `hooks.enable`. Use `disable_after_run` for a terminal condition. Hooks have no connector credentials. Do not add secrets to a detector.

Both mechanisms poll. Changes can be missed between checks, and schedules can run late. They do not provide continuous observation or block source events.

## Services in this runtime

The guest has systemd but boots a minimal `default.target`, not the usual `multi-user.target` or a login session. Prefer the existing guest system manager. Connect startup to the target the guest actually reaches. Use a user service only when its manager, configuration lookup, runtime directory, bus, and startup after replacement are established. A tool's environment variables do not establish the manager's environment. Do not assume lingering works or use host operator facilities.

Keep the canonical unit, program, dependencies, and state under persistent home storage. Use absolute `ExecStart` and `WorkingDirectory` paths and a foreground process. Set an appropriate `Restart` policy, `RestartSec`, and start limits. Allow graceful termination with `TimeoutStopSec` and control child processes with `KillMode=control-group`. Bound resources and logs. Match restart behavior to the program. Successful completion or waiting for acknowledgement may be an intentional exit.

Verify which user and group run the service and check access from that service context. Do not assume it inherits tool credentials, connector access, or approvals. Use supported access paths. Do not copy secrets into units, arguments, environment files, or logs.

## Recovery after replacement

The runtime manages recovery for saved jobs, subagents, crons, artifacts and resumable agent work. Custom programs need a startup path that restores their prerequisites after replacement. Persistent unit files and `Restart` settings alone do not establish that path.

Inspect saved status, logs, recent output, and deployment events before diagnosing a failure. A quiet listener may be waiting for acknowledgement. A failed bootstrap may have partly succeeded. Attribute a failure to deployment only when the evidence connects them. Check interruption and recovery failures separately.

Use the supported startup path. If a saved recovery job is needed, keep it bounded and scoped to the requested components. Its interval provides another attempt, not a recovery deadline.

When recovering work, follow these steps.

1. Read durable desired state before starting anything. Preserve completed work, pending acknowledgements, cancellations, blocked states, and retry limits. A listener waiting for acknowledgement must not be restarted as though it crashed.
2. Restore required prerequisites and verify them. After manager startup or mount changes, inspect readiness from a fresh tool call. Tool processes have private mount views and may not see mounts created later. Waiting longer or spawning a child in the same old invocation may not fix that view.
3. Reconcile each component independently. Inspect running state before starting another copy. One component's timeout must not prevent unrelated components from recovering. Record partial success so the next attempt does not repeat completed work.
4. Resume from checkpoints and verify useful progress. Write checkpoints atomically and regularly. Abrupt termination can skip shutdown handlers. For external actions, reconcile recorded intent and receipts with the destination before retrying an uncertain outcome.

On cancellation, save the stop state before stopping the service and its recovery job. Recovery must not undo the user's decision.

## Verification and delivery

Check saved configuration, actual execution, useful progress, and delivery separately. For crons, use `cron.status` and `cron.runs`, then verify that any owed result reached the conversation. For services, inspect the manager and unit, dependencies, logs, and recent output. An active unit or existing PID alone does not establish health.

Test the failure mode you claim to handle. Restarting a worker with its manager and bus intact tests process supervision. Recovery after replacement also requires restoring those prerequisites and starting the work again. Do not infer the latter from the former. Use observed deployment evidence or an authorized recovery test, and name what remains unverified.

Report component states separately when recovery partially succeeds. A failed bootstrap does not prove that every service is down. A quiet listener does not prove a crash. Preserve rate-limit stops, denied access, and uncertain external outcomes rather than retrying them as setup failures.

Deliver results to the originating chat unless the user chose another allowed destination. Provider-connected side chats retain their exact-chat boundary. Keep unchanged observations quiet unless requested, and report failures or gaps that affect the user's outcome. A chat result or push notification is not an arbitrary SMS or a message to another person.
